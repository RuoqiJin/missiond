# Can 2026 AI Video Models Count Machine-Gun Shots From Body Recoil Alone? A Capability Assessment

## TL;DR
- **No general-purpose multimodal model in June 2026 — including the model you mean by "Gemini 3.5 Flash" (Google's real flagship Flash model, generally available since May 19, 2026) — can reliably count individual machine-gun shots from visual recoil/body-shake alone.** The task fails on a hard physics/engineering limit: a machine gun fires ~12–17 rounds per second, but these models sample video at 1 frame per second by default and cap custom sampling at 24 fps, which is at or below the Nyquist rate needed to even detect events that fast.
- **The right tool is a purpose-built temporal repetition-counting (RAC) computer-vision model (e.g., RepNet, TransRAC, ESCounts, CountLLM) running on high-frame-rate footage — not a chat LLM.** Even these specialists are designed for slow, periodic human actions (1–5 reps/sec like push-ups) and cap their detectable repetition rate at the Nyquist limit (RepNet's shortest resolvable period is 2 frames); none is validated on 10+ events/second firing.
- **A practical pipeline could approximate the count today, but only with caveats:** capture or obtain genuine high-speed video (240+ fps), and either count muzzle flashes/ejected casings with a detector or — more reliably — derive the count arithmetically from firing duration × the weapon's known cyclic rate. Counting purely from "背影抖动" (the shake of the person's back) is the least reliable signal and is not something any 2026 model does accurately.

## Key Findings

**1. Model-name correction.** There is no real "Gemini 3.5 Flash" mistake to worry about — Google *does* now have a model by almost exactly that name. As of June 1, 2026, **Gemini 3.5 Flash** is real and is Google's newest, generally-available Flash-tier model (announced at Google I/O on May 19, 2026, GA since then). It sits in a lineup that also includes **Gemini 3 Pro**, **Gemini 3.1 Pro** (flagship pure-reasoning model, released February 2026), **Gemini 3 Flash**, **Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite**, and a generative video model called **Gemini Omni**. So "a model as strong as Gemini 3.5 Flash" is a coherent, current reference point — it is a frontier model with native video understanding. The user's naming was essentially correct.

**2. The competitor landscape (June 2026).** All three frontier labs ship natively-multimodal models that ingest video: Google's **Gemini 3.5 Flash / 3.1 Pro**; OpenAI's **GPT-5.5** (released April 23, 2026, described as "natively omnimodal," processing text, images, audio, and video in one unified architecture); and Anthropic's **Claude Opus 4.7** (April 16, 2026) and **Opus 4.8**. Among open models, Alibaba's **Qwen3-VL** series is the most relevant for video, with explicit "text-timestamp alignment" for temporal grounding (replacing T-RoPE). All of them, however, share the same architectural bottleneck for *this* task (below).

**3. The frame-rate bottleneck is decisive.** Gemini's video pipeline samples video at **1 frame per second (FPS) by default**, confirmed by Google's official Gemini API docs ("By default 1 frame per second (FPS) is sampled from the video"). Google's own documentation warns verbatim: **"Be aware that fast action sequences might lose detail due to the 1 FPS sampling rate."** You can raise the sampling rate via a custom `fps` parameter, but the **maximum allowed value is 24 FPS** (the documented range is `(0.0, 24.0]`, per Google's Vertex AI REST API reference and corroborated by Google Cloud Community author Laurent Picard). Gemini 3 Pro is "optimized" for up to **10 FPS** in Google's own demos (10× the default), not 60. The real-time Live API is hard-capped at 1 FPS — Google explicitly states this makes it **"unsuitable for use cases that require analyzing fast-changing video, such as play-by-play in high-speed sports."**

**4. The arithmetic doesn't work for full-auto fire.** A machine gun's cyclic rate is roughly **700–850 rounds/minute** for an M249 SAW — per U.S. military spec MIL-DTL-70446C §3.13: *"The cyclic rate of fire for each M249 machine gun with its main and assigned barrel shall be between 700 and 850 rounds per minute"* (~12–14 rounds/second; the original dual gas regulator allowed up to 1,000 rpm, ~17/sec). The meme's minigun fires a fictionalized 10,000 rpm = ~166/second. To *count discrete events* you need a sampling rate at least 2× the event rate (Nyquist). At 24 FPS — the absolute Gemini ceiling — you can resolve at most ~12 distinct events/second, which is **below** a real machine gun's rate and astronomically below a minigun's. At the 1 FPS default, the task is hopeless. Worse: even at 24 FPS, a standard 24–30 fps source video doesn't *contain* the information — capturing recoil cleanly requires high-speed cameras (240 fps on modern phones; 1,000+ fps for true ballistic recoil analysis).

**5. Multimodal LLMs are independently bad at *counting* in video, regardless of frame rate.** This is documented across benchmarks. On **TemporalBench**, models "struggle most on action frequency (counting)." On the 2026 **EC-Bench** (counting in long videos), the paper reports: *"Across 22 multimodal large language models (MLLMs), the best model achieves only 29.98% accuracy on Enumeration and 23.74% on Counting, while human performance reaches 78.57% and 82.97%, respectively."* So the weakness is two-fold: they can't *see* fast events, and even when events are visible they can't reliably *tally* them.

**6. Specialized RAC models are the actual state of the art — but only for slow actions.** The dedicated computer-vision task is "Repetitive Action Counting (RAC)," with benchmarks **RepCount, UCFRep, Countix**. Leading methods: **RepNet** (Google, class-agnostic), **TransRAC** (transformer, CVPR 2022), **ESCounts** (exemplar-based), and **CountLLM** (CVPR 2025, LLM-based, current SOTA). Per the CountLLM paper, on the RepCount dataset with a VideoMAE encoder it *"achieves an OBO accuracy of 0.639 and an MAE of 0.162, significantly surpassing TransRAC (0.410 OBO) and ESCounts (0.563 OBO)."* These are designed for fitness reps and similar **1–5 Hz** motions — and RepNet's architecture predicts period lengths only down to **2 frames** (the Nyquist floor). None is built or validated for ~12–166 events/second.

## Details

### What "Gemini 3.5 Flash" actually is and what it can do with video
Google released **Gemini 3.5 Flash** (stable model string `gemini-3.5-flash`) on May 19, 2026, positioning it as frontier-intelligence-at-Flash-speed that "leads in multimodal understanding (84.2% on CharXiv Reasoning)" and is natively multimodal across text, audio, images, code, and video, with a 1M-token context window (64K-token output). It can ingest video up to roughly an hour at default media resolution (1M context), or three hours at low resolution. For the *kind* of "counting video" you describe — real footage with a post-production cost tally overlaid — Gemini can absolutely (a) recognize that a soldier is firing a machine gun, (b) read and transcribe any on-screen text, and (c) reason about cost arithmetic if given the cyclic rate. What it *cannot* do is independently and precisely count each individual shot from the subtle recoil of the shooter's body.

### Why the recoil-shake signal specifically is the hardest path
Three independent problems stack up:
1. **Temporal resolution.** Even with custom sampling at the 24 FPS maximum, you are at or below the Nyquist rate for a 12–17 rounds/sec weapon. Consecutive recoil impulses blur into a single continuous shudder; the model literally does not receive distinct frames per shot.
2. **Signal subtlety.** "背影抖动" (shaking of the person's back/silhouette) is a second-order, low-amplitude cue — far weaker than a muzzle flash or an ejected casing. RAC research repeatedly notes that camera motion, motion blur, and viewpoint changes degrade counting; a handheld combat clip is a worst case.
3. **Counting weakness.** As EC-Bench and TemporalBench show, current MLLMs are poor at numerically aggregating repeated events even when each is individually visible.

### What specialized CV can and can't do
The RAC literature is the correct frame of reference. **RepNet** builds a temporal self-similarity matrix from per-frame embeddings and predicts per-frame periodicity; it is class-agnostic, so in principle it could attempt to count any periodic motion, including recoil. But its period-length predictor outputs discrete classes only in the range {2, …, 32} frames for a 64-frame input window — i.e., the **minimum detectable period is 2 frames (the Nyquist limit)**, extendable to *longer* periods (up to 160 frames via 5× multi-speed playback), *not* shorter ones. So at, say, 30 fps input, RepNet's fastest countable cadence is ~15 reps/sec — and that is the theoretical ceiling, not a validated operating point for gunfire. To count a 166/sec minigun you would need input footage well above ~332 fps and a model retrained for that regime; nothing off-the-shelf does this. The fitness datasets these models are tuned on (RepCount, UCFRep, Countix) average only a handful of seconds per repetition.

### The pragmatic pipeline that *would* work
For someone actually wanting to auto-generate these "cost-per-shot" videos:
- **Best approach (arithmetic).** Detect the *start* and *end* of each firing burst (trivial for any model, even at 1 fps, and audio makes it easier) and multiply burst duration by the weapon's published cyclic rate (e.g., M249 ≈ 800 rpm). This yields an accurate round count without counting individual shots — and it matches exactly how the original "$400,000 for 12 seconds" *Team Fortress 2* minigun meme was computed (10,000 rpm × 0.2 min × $200/round).
- **Second-best (muzzle flash / casing detection).** A trained object detector (YOLO-class) on genuine high-speed footage (240+ fps) can detect discrete muzzle flashes or ejected casings frame-by-frame, then tally them. Muzzle-flash detection is established technology used in fielded gunshot-detection systems.
- **Worst approach (recoil shake).** What you specifically asked about — counting from body-shake alone — is the least reliable signal and is not something any 2026 model does well.

## Recommendations
1. **Do not use a chat LLM (Gemini 3.5 Flash, GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7) to count the shots.** Use it for the parts it is genuinely good at: identifying the weapon, transcribing on-screen text, narrating, and doing the cost math. **Threshold to revisit:** if a future model documents reliable counting at >60 fps effective sampling *and* high off-by-one accuracy on a fast-event benchmark, re-evaluate.
2. **For the count itself, use the duration × cyclic-rate method.** It is robust, explainable, and matches the meme's own internal logic. Have the LLM detect burst start/end timestamps (it can do this even at low fps) and supply the weapon's rpm.
3. **If you insist on per-shot visual counting, build a two-stage CV pipeline on high-speed source video:** (a) ensure footage is ≥240 fps, (b) run a fine-tuned muzzle-flash/casing detector (or a RAC model retrained for high cadence), (c) de-duplicate and tally. Treat recoil-shake as a fallback signal only.
4. **Set accuracy expectations.** Even specialized RAC models on *slow* actions are only "off-by-one" correct ~56–64% of the time on hard datasets (ESCounts 0.563, CountLLM 0.639 OBO on RepCount). Frame-precise gunshot counting from visuals alone should be considered research-grade, not production-ready, in 2026.

## Caveats
- **Vendor docs and FPS ceilings can change.** Google explicitly notes sampling rates "are subject to change in the future for improvements in inference." The 24 FPS maximum is the documented ceiling as of June 2026; if Google raises it, the Nyquist math improves but does not, by itself, fix the counting weakness or the need for high-speed source footage.
- **Source quality varies.** Several model-capability claims come from vendor blogs and aggregator sites; the quantitative benchmark numbers (EC-Bench, RepCount/CountLLM) are from peer-reviewed/arXiv papers and are more reliable. I have flagged the distinction throughout.
- **The minigun meme uses fictional figures.** The "$400,000 / 10,000 rpm / 12 seconds" line is from Valve's *Team Fortress 2* "Meet the Heavy" and is not a real weapon's spec; real machine-gun cyclic rates (700–1,000 rpm) are far lower, and even fielded miniguns top out near 2,000–6,000 rpm.
- **"As strong as Gemini 3.5 Flash" is a moving target.** Gemini 3.5 Pro was announced as in testing and "coming next month" (~June 2026); a stronger general model will not change the fundamental sampling/Nyquist limitation for this specific high-cadence counting task. The path forward is a specialized pipeline, not a bigger chatbot.
